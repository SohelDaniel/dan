# Fix the tutor with code before training models

The short answer is **don't train a model yet, and never train one to write whole scenes.** Your eval data shows Claude's real weak spots are of three kinds. Some are mechanical: wrong coordinates, overlapping or leftover labels, colours that don't match, cue tags that are never spoken. Plain code can fix all of these without touching creativity. One is a planning gap: Claude narrates a step or a number and never draws it. This happens in **30 of 44** current-prompt scenes, and code can catch it before the beat plays. The last kind is judgement: picking a good picture for physical subjects like the heart, chemical bonds or circuits, and getting facts right. Only a frontier model does this well, and a small model trained on your **335 judged scenes (44 distinct questions)** would copy Claude's average output and lose breadth. The speed case for a small model is also weak on your Mac. Scaled from the notes' M4 Max figures to your base M5 (roughly a third of the memory bandwidth), a 7-8B local model would need about **20-25 seconds to write a 600-token scene**, longer than Claude's current ~18 s for a whole turn. The plan: first spend a few weeks on deterministic fixes and a small library of picture templates for physical topics, with Claude still choosing. Keep Claude's thinking (you already measured that it buys accuracy). Start saving your own redo/reject choices as free preference labels. Train something small only if a narrow, label-rich job is still failing after that: a 1.5-3B "number-binding repair" tagger or a personal taste critic. Either costs well under $100 and trains in hours on your Mac.

## Claude fails most on "spoken but not shown", not on syntax

The audit covers 308 judged scenes: 7 prompt and engine variants of the same 44 questions, each scored 1-5 by a Sonnet judge on seven dimensions. The weakest dimension in every run is **binding**, meaning the narration says something the picture doesn't show. It averages **3.06**, against 3.43 for layout, 3.48 for the picture, 3.62 for teaching and 3.63 for accuracy. Fit and naturalness (about 3.9-4.0) are effectively solved ([all.json](file://<scratchpad>/audit/all.json)). Only **36 of 308 scenes (11.7%)** score 4 or more on every dimension, and in the current prompt run (latA) only 4 of 44 do: dijkstra, git, hashtable and recursion ([latA summary](file://<scratchpad>/latA/summary.json)).

The weak spots cluster by subject, and the clustering explains what kind of help is needed. CS algorithms score about 4.0-4.25: graphs, trees and arrays, which your engine's graph layout handles and where Claude only names nodes and links. The worst questions are physical things that must be drawn from dots, lines and shapes: **bonds 2.98, refraction 3.04, circuits 3.08, pythagoras 3.12, pH 3.22, DNA 3.24, heart 3.27**. "Why" questions are the weakest question type ([all.json](file://<scratchpad>/audit/all.json)). The same failure comes back on the same question across runs. The heart is "four boxes with no vessels, valves or arrows" in one run and "a crude grid of boxes" in another. These are real weak spots, not judge noise (the judge's noise is about ±0.15 on a mean) ([latA heart score](file://<scratchpad>/latA/full/heart/score.json)).

All 44 latA judge notes were hand-sorted into failure types; one scene can have several. The counts:

| Failure | Scenes (of 44) | Who can fix it |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken but not shown (a step, claim or number narrated, never drawn) | **30** | Code detects it; deciding *what* to draw is judgement |
| Overlap, detached or clipped labels | 20 | Engine |
| Generic picture ("boxes instead of the thing") | 17 (10 strict) | Judgement; partly templates |
| Claude's own coordinate or arithmetic errors | **12** | Code |
| Leftover clutter in the final frame | 12 | Code |
| Colours that don't mean anything or don't match the formula | 11 | Code |
| Drawn before or after it is said | 8 | Mostly code |
| Wrong facts in the narration | 4-6 | Judgement |
| Markup that failed to parse | 1 | Code |

A keyword pass over the other six runs gives the same ranking ([cats.py](file://<scratchpad>/audit/cats.py)). By instance, **about half the failures are purely mechanical, about a third need judgement**, and the rest are mixed. Syntax is not the problem: 39-43 of 44 scenes decode in every run, and real chats look the same (32 of 36 staged turns fine) ([chat sessions](file://<repo>/chat/sessions)).

The binding failure is a gap between plan and execution, not a lack of intent. In the attention scene, Claude's plan says "softmax, sums to 1 → links morph to weights; mix of values → send from words to it", but the judge notes "the blend step has no weighted output shown" ([attention plan](file://<scratchpad>/latA/full/attention/plan.txt)). This matters for the training question. Claude already knows what it should show. What's missing is a check that holds it to its plan *before* the beat plays. Today the binding lints feed only the *next* turn. That also explains why dropping the plan block raised these lints from 41 to 54 at equal judge scores.

## Coordinates and clutter belong to code

The numeric failures are the clearest case for plain code. **18 of 44 latA scenes contain coordinates Claude typed by hand**, and in 6 of 7 runs those scenes score lower on accuracy, by 0.09-0.46. In the "don't deliberate" run (latB) the gap is widest: 3.06 vs 3.52 ([audit scripts](file://<scratchpad>/audit)). The errors are concrete. Claude drew the supply-and-demand shortage at x=3.5 when the curve gives 2 ([supply played.txt](file://<scratchpad>/latA/full/supply/played.txt)). It put the catalyst's energy bar at x=6.5, away from the peak ([catalyst played.txt](file://<scratchpad>/latA/full/catalyst/played.txt)). The pendulum's pull arrows are not along the path, and in three runs the "overfit" curve doesn't miss the new point enough to make its point.

Outside research agrees with your data. Language models make "logical and arithmetic mistakes in the solution part, even when the problem is decomposed correctly". Letting the model write the relation and a program compute the answer beat chain-of-thought by 15 points on grade-school maths ([PAL, arXiv 2211.10435](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.10435)). Coordinate and grid benchmarks show models breaking down as the size or the number of constraints grows ([Stuck in the Matrix, arXiv 2510.20198](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.20198); [FloorplanQA, arXiv 2507.07644](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.07644v1)). The best 2025-2026 animation systems all take layout and computation away from the language model. In ALGOGEN, the model writes semantics and a deterministic renderer does the layout: it reaches **99.8% success against 82.5%** for direct Manim code ([ALGOGEN, arXiv 2605.12159](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12159)). A symbolic checker that reads the scene graph without rendering was preferred by humans over a vision-model critic 65% of the time ([SGA, arXiv 2607.18116](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18116)). Your engine already *is* that architecture. The fix is to extend it, not to add a model.

In practice, the markup should let Claude *name* geometric facts instead of computing them, for example "the intersection of demand and supply at price 5", "the peak of this curve", "the tangent to the path here" or "an auto-fitted plot range". Then the server or engine computes them exactly. This keeps all of Claude's creative choices (what to plot, what to point at) and removes only the arithmetic. It may also shorten thinking, since Claude stops working out intersections in its head. The other mechanical failures have equally direct fixes, all already suggested by your own lints:

- **Clutter:** annotations expire at the end of their sentence, and faded "ghosts" get a cap.
- **Colour:** formula colour names are cross-checked against things on stage, and things that share an identity share a colour.
- **Cue tags:** the cue pre-flight's fuzzy matching is widened so a tag that is never spoken gets glued to a nearby word.
- **Parser:** it accepts a bare `check`/`cross` as an annotation, and brackets are checked before playback.
- **Render bugs:** subscript glyphs the font lacks, unrounded `\val` output, and the session bug where the previous turn's lint text was parsed as scene markup ([session 20261020-223156](file://<repo>/chat/sessions/20261020-223156/messages.jsonl)).

For binding itself, code can do more than it does now. Two changes:

- **Run the lints on each beat as it streams in, before it plays.** The lint already says what's wrong and even gives the fix ("put the number where it happens").
- **Compare the plan's "show:" pairs with what the beat actually stages.**

Two caveats apply:

- The current binding lint agrees only weakly with the judge's binding score (correlation −0.20 over 308 scenes), so it catches spoken numbers better than missing processes.
- What to do when a beat fails has no free answer. Inserting a label automatically is cheap. Asking Claude to redo the beat costs seconds.

## The judgement gaps are exactly where small models are weakest

What's left after code is the creative core. One part is choosing a picture that shows the real thing: a heart with vessels and valves, electrons shared *between* nuclei, a ray bending at a boundary. The other parts are deciding how to animate a narrated process ("show the doubling"), and stating facts carefully ("pH is H⁺ concentration, not acid strength"). The research consistently says this is frontier-model territory. In Code2Video's comparison, **Claude Opus produced the most attractive videos and the best "does it teach" score**, while other strong models wrote leaner but less appealing code ([Code2Video ICML 2026 slides](https://icml.cc/media/icml-2026/Slides/65050.pdf)). The only published small fine-tuned Manim models reach **62% render success on 417 training examples**, against 82-94% for frontier agent pipelines with retries ([ManimBench](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/399370691_Large_Language_Model_Approaches_to_Educational_Video_Generation_Using_Manim); [TheoremExplainAgent, arXiv 2502.19400](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19400)).

Where small models do beat frontier ones, the job has three traits: a narrow output language, 100k-200k+ paired examples, and a target image to compare against. Examples are TikZ figures, SVG icons and chart-to-code ([DeTikZify, arXiv 2405.15306](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.15306); [VisCoder, arXiv 2506.03930](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.03930)). Explaining a concept has no target image, so none of the three fits. Those big datasets are also usually distilled from a frontier model, so the student mostly copies its teacher ([ChartMaster, arXiv 2508.17608](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.17608v1)). On creativity, fine-tuning moves the output's variety toward the variety of the training data ([arXiv 2609.16454](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.16454v1)), and structured output formats reduce variety on their own ([The Price of Format, arXiv 2505.18949](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18949v1)). A student trained on a few hundred Claude scenes would therefore learn a handful of templates. That is the opposite of what the weak topics need. No study measures teaching quality in small versus frontier models for visual explanations, so this is a well-grounded inference, not a measured result.

There is a cheap way to help Claude on the generic-picture problem without taking the choice away: a **small library of domain templates** in the engine or as worked examples. A heart with chambers and vessels, an atom with shells, a ray diagram, a circuit loop, a ladder scale, a double helix. Claude still decides whether to use one and what to show with it, but it no longer has to build a heart out of polygons. This targets the bottom of your table (bonds, refraction, circuits, pH, DNA, heart), and nobody has to train anything. Your own best scenes (recursion, bayes, tcp, dijkstra, interference) can be handed back to Claude as examples in the same way.

Thinking also stays with Claude. Your own measurement settles it. "Don't deliberate" cut the first sentence from about 7.5 s to about 4 s, but accuracy fell **3.70 → 3.34**, teaching 3.68 → 3.41 and binding 3.18 → 2.80. In real chats the first sentence arrives at a median of **7.9 s** and the turn finishes at 18.3 s ([chat sessions](file://<repo>/chat/sessions)). A small "fast talker" that says an opening line while Claude thinks is an established pattern ([Talker-Reasoner, arXiv 2410.08328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08328v1)), but it risks the opening contradicting what follows. Published work keeps such openers to framing, not substance. Treat it as an optional experiment, not a fix.

## Your data is thin, and your Mac makes a local scene writer slow

**Data.** You have about **335 judged question → scene pairs**. Each comes with its plan, reply, lint output, layout score, 2-3 frames and a short critique. Only **36** are good on every dimension, and they cover just 44 distinct questions. There are also 39 unjudged real-chat turns. Repeated asks are a signal of their own: "he initialization" was asked 7+ times across 5 sessions. All labels come from a low-effort Sonnet judge that also sees the lints, so they are noisy and partly echo the lints. No outside corpus can replace this. The 3Blue1Brown code is coordinate-based Manim with no question attached to each scene, Hugging Face prompt→Manim sets are around 3k rows of unknown quality ([BechusRantus/manim-code-dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/BechusRantus/manim-code-dataset/blob/main/README.md)), and the large diagram sets are static images without narration ([AI2D](https://prior.allenai.org/projects/diagram-understanding); [ScienceQA, arXiv 2209.09513](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.09513)). Each would need an LLM pass to translate it into your markup, plus your checker as a filter. That pattern works for code ([MultiPL-T, arXiv 2308.09895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09895)), but nobody has measured it for a semantic animation language.

The cheap data source is Claude itself: generate, run your checker and the judge, and keep the best of several tries. This is known as rejection sampling, and it is how distilled students end up above their teacher's average ([ReST-EM, arXiv 2312.06585](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06585); [s1, arXiv 2501.19393](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.19393)). At October 2026 prices, Sonnet 5.5 is $2 in / $10 out per million tokens and Haiku 5.5 is $0.10 / $0.50 (Claude API reference, cached 2026-10-06). The notes estimate about **$25-35 per 1,000 raw Sonnet scenes**, about $10 per 1,000 for judging, and **$100-300 in total for 1-2k good pairs**, assuming a 30-60% keep rate. You can measure that rate cheaply with 100 questions × 4 tries. For a narrow sub-task the examples are short, so the cost drops to tens of dollars. A whole-scene model would need thousands to tens of thousands of scenes: grammar-constrained decoding makes the *syntax* almost free even on 2-7B models ([SynCode, arXiv 2403.01632](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.01632)), but choosing the right marks and cues is what needs data.

**Your Mac.** The notes' speed figures are for an M4 Max with 410-546 GB/s of memory bandwidth. Your base M5 has about **153 GB/s** ([Apple M5 announcement](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/10/apple-unleashes-m5-the-next-big-leap-in-ai-performance-for-apple-silicon/)). Generating text on a Mac is limited mainly by bandwidth, so expect roughly **one third of M4 Max generation speed**. The M5's new GPU neural accelerators mainly speed up reading the prompt (time to first token), not writing tokens ([Apple ML research on MLX and M5](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/exploring-llms-mlx-m5)). Scaling the notes' llama.cpp figures (7-8B at 4-bit: 70-83 tok/s on M4 Max), plus MLX's usual 1.3-1.8× edge ([MLX vs llama.cpp](https://www.compute-market.com/blog/mlx-vs-llama-cpp-apple-silicon-2026)), gives these estimates for your machine:

| Model (4-bit) | Generation speed on M5 (est.) | 600-token whole scene | 30-token fix per beat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7-8B | ~22-35 tok/s | **~18-27 s** | ~1-1.5 s |
| 3-4B | ~45-70 tok/s | ~9-13 s | ~0.5-0.7 s |
| 1-1.5B | ~100-150 tok/s | ~4-6 s | **~0.2-0.3 s** |

These are extrapolations. Run `mlx_lm.generate --verbose` once to get real numbers. A hosted 8B model on Cerebras would write a scene in about 0.3 s ([Cerebras pricing](https://www.morphllm.com/cerebras-pricing)), but that buys speed with a model that is worse at the creative work. The table shows that **a local small model is only fast for small jobs**: a few dozen tokens per beat, run alongside Claude's stream. It is not a replacement for Claude's scene.

**Training on your Mac.** The notes cite about 380 tok/s for 8B LoRA on an M4 Max (blog numbers, unverified) ([InsiderLLM MLX guide](https://insiderllm.com/guides/fine-tuning-mac-lora-mlx/)). LoRA is a cheap fine-tuning method that trains a small add-on instead of the whole model. Training depends on raw GPU compute, and your 10-core GPU has a quarter of the M4 Max's 40 cores, so assume **3-5× slower**. 1,000 whole scenes (about 1.5k tokens each, 3 passes) would take roughly **10-15 hours for 8B** or 3-5 hours for 3B. A narrow tagger trained on short examples (about 300 tokens each) takes about **1-2 hours for a 1.5-3B model**. Memory is fine: 8B LoRA needs about 14 GB, and the 4-bit version (QLoRA) about 7 GB, both well inside your 32 GB. The same job on a rented H100 costs about **$2-4 per run** ([RunPod pricing](https://www.runpod.io/gpu-cloud/pricing)), so money doesn't decide it; convenience does. Claude itself can't usefully be fine-tuned: only Claude 3 Haiku on Bedrock is offered ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fine-tune-claude-3-haiku-ga)).

## What to do, in order

| Step | What | Done by | Data needed | Cost / time (est.) | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Geometric references (intersection, peak, tangent, auto range) so Claude never types coordinates | Code (server/engine) | none | days of work, $0 | Removes most of the 12/44 numeric errors; probably shortens thinking |
| 2 | Per-beat pre-flight: run binding/hook/colour lints as each beat streams, before it plays; auto-insert the label for a spoken number; check the plan's "show:" pairs | Code | none | days; adds milliseconds | Attacks the 30/44 binding failures at the point they happen; may let the plan block go without the 41→54 rise |
| 3 | Auto-expire annotations, cap ghosts, colour cross-check, wider cue fuzzy-matching, forgiving parser, render bugs, lint-leak bug | Code / engine | none | days | Clears clutter (12), colour (11), timing (8), parse and render failures |
| 4 | Template library for physical topics (heart, atom/bond, ray, circuit, ladder, helix) plus your best scenes as examples | Engine + prompt; **Claude chooses** | your 36 all-≥4 scenes | a week; $0 | Targets the bottom-scoring questions without touching creativity |
| 5 | Re-run the 44-question eval after each step | Claude + judge | existing | a few dollars per run | Tells you what's actually left |
| 6 | Log every turn plus your redo/reject choices as preference pairs | Code | grows on its own | $0 | Builds the only data that reflects *your* taste |
| 7 (only if still needed) | **Binding-repair tagger**: given a beat, which mark gets which spoken number or "twice as big" | Small model, Qwen-class 1.5-3B, LoRA on Mac | 1-2k examples made by Claude, filtered by your lints | ~$20-60 of API, 1-2 h training | ~0.2-0.7 s per beat, alongside the stream; handles fuzzy cases code misses |
| 8 (optional) | **Personal taste critic**: small scorer on frozen image embeddings, ranks scene variants for offline evals | Small model | a few hundred of your own picks, pre-sorted by the Claude judge | minutes to train | A cheaper, less noisy stand-in for the low-effort Sonnet judge in evals; too slow for live best-of-N |

Claude keeps the plan, the choice of picture and metaphor, how a process is shown, the facts, and its thinking time. The one small model with a real case is the step-7 tagger, because its job is narrow, your lints can label its examples automatically, and its output is a few tokens. Even it should wait until steps 1-3 show how much deterministic auto-insertion leaves behind. The personal critic in step 8 is cheap but has modest expected gains: personalised preference models at much larger scale improved only from 0.40 to 0.45 in agreement with unseen users' rankings ([arXiv 2604.07427](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.07427)). Build it only if the Sonnet judge's noise starts to block prompt decisions.

## Conclusion

The question "what is Claude bad at that code can't do" has a smaller answer than the raw failure counts suggest. Most of what lowers your scores is Claude being asked to do things it shouldn't do at all, like arithmetic on coordinates and cleaning up after itself. Another large share is Claude failing to follow its own plan, which code can hold it to while the beat is still on its way. What's truly left is depicting physical things and stating facts carefully. Those are the skills that make Claude worth its 8 seconds of thinking, and a small model trained on 44 questions is weakest at exactly those. Your Mac's bandwidth settles the speed question: locally, a small model wins only on jobs measured in tens of tokens.

One uncertainty matters more than the rest. Every number here comes from a low-effort Sonnet judge that also sees your lints, with no ratings from you. Step 6 is cheap and it is the only data that measures your taste, not Claude's, so start logging your choices now.
